1.1 Decomposition of the Residual Error

NoteMathematical Setup & Random Variables
Quantity Variable / Expression Expectation & Properties
Truth \(f(x)\) \(\mathbb{E}[f(x)] = f(x)\) (unknown,deterministic function of \(x\) and possibly further variables)
Noise \(\varepsilon\) \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = \sigma^2\)
Observed Target \(Y = f(x) + \varepsilon\) \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = f(x) + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = f(x)\)
Model Prediction \(\hat{Y}\) \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]\) (expected model prediction across training datasets)
Residual Error \(Y - \hat{Y}\) \(\mathbb{E}[Y - \hat{Y}]\) (expected difference between target and prediction

Decomposition of the Residual Error

The residual error, \(Y - \hat{Y}\), is a random variable that can be decomposed into three distinct components: Bias, Variance, and Irreducible Noise:

\[\begin{aligned} Y - \hat{Y} &= \big(f(x) + \varepsilon\big) - \hat{Y} \\ &= \underbrace{\big(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]\big)}_{B} + \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}] - \hat{Y}\big)}_{V} + \varepsilon \\ &= B + V + \varepsilon \end{aligned}\]

where:

  • bias \(B = f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]\) measures the systematic deviation between the true underlying mechanism \(f(x)\) and the expected prediction of the model across datasets. Notice that \(B\) is a deterministic constant.
  • variance \(V = \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}] - \hat{Y}\) captures the sample-to-sample fluctuation of the estimator \(\hat{Y}\) around its own expectation (with \(\mathbb{E}[V] = 0\)).
  • \(\varepsilon = Y - f(x)\) is the irreducible noise in the target observation (with \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\)).

Mean Squared Error and the Pythagorean Relation

The Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the expected squared residual error:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{MSE} & = \mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] \\ & = \mathbb{E}\left[(B + V + \varepsilon)^2\right] \\ & = \mathbb{E}[B^2] + \mathbb{E}[V^2] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] \\ &\quad + 2\,\mathbb{E}[B V] + 2\,\mathbb{E}[B \varepsilon] + 2\,\mathbb{E}[V \varepsilon] \end{aligned}\]

All three cross-terms vanish:

  1. Bias and Variance: Because \(B\) is a deterministic constant, it factors out of the expectation: \[\mathbb{E}[B V] = B \cdot \mathbb{E}[V] = B \cdot \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}] - \hat{Y}] = B \cdot 0 = 0\]

  2. Bias and Noise: Because \(B\) is constant and the noise has zero mean: \[\mathbb{E}[B \varepsilon] = B \cdot \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = B \cdot 0 = 0\]

  3. Variance and Noise: Because the test noise \(\varepsilon\) is independent of the training data used to form \(\hat{Y}\): \[\mathbb{E}[V \varepsilon] = \mathbb{E}[V] \cdot \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0\]

NotePythagoras again: Orthogonality of Random Variables

If we define the inner product of two random variables as \(\langle U, W \rangle = \mathbb{E}[U W]\), then \(\mathbb{E}[U W] = 0\) is equivalent to orthogonality of the two random variables. So the decomposition of the MSE is reminiscent of the Pythagorean theorem.

\[\boxed{\text{MSE} = \mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] = \underbrace{(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}])^2}_{\text{Bias}^2(\hat{Y})} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{Y} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}])^2\right]}_{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{Y})} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible Noise}}}\]

2. \(L_2\) Regularization (Ridge)

Because irreducible noise \(\sigma^2\) is an unchangeable property, reducing total prediction error requires balancing Bias and Variance:

  • Standard OLS fits training data without constraints. For flexible models (such as high-degree polynomials), OLS achieves zero or near-zero training bias (\(\text{Bias}^2 \approx 0\)), but can result in large Variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{Y})\) where predictions swing wildly from one sample (e.g. patient cohort) to another.

  • The Regularization Principle: Rather than insisting on strict unbiasedness, the goal of regularization is to deliberately accept a small, controlled amount of Bias by constraining parameter magnitudes, in exchange for a large reduction in Variance.

By accepting a slight bias, we stabilize the estimator against sample-to-sample volatility and achieve a substantially lower overall Mean Squared Error.

2.1 The Ridge Estimator

Ridge Regression (\(L_2\) Regularization) operationalizes this principle by adding a penalty on the squared Euclidean norm of the parameter vector to the loss function:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2\]

where:

  • \(\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\theta})^2\) is the standard Residual Sum of Squares (RSS).

  • \(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^p \theta_j^2\) is the squared Euclidean norm of the parameter vector.

  • \(\lambda \ge 0\) is the regularization hyperparameter:

    • \(\lambda = 0\): Recovers standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS).
    • \(\lambda \to \infty\): Drives all weights toward zero (\(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \to \mathbf{0}\)).

Minimizing the regularized loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) yields the modified normal equations in closed form:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}\]

Notice the algebraic effect of \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\):

  • When model space is large, the Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) is near-singular and poorly conditioned.

  • Adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) shifts every diagonal entry by \(+\lambda\), guaranteeing that \((\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\) is strictly positive-definite and invertible for any \(\lambda > 0\).

3. \(L_1\) Regularization (Lasso)

While Ridge shrinks coefficients continuously toward zero, it rarely drives any parameter strictly to zero. Lasso Regression (\(L_1\) Regularization) replaces the squared penalty with the sum of absolute values:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lasso}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1 = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p |\theta_j|\]

Because the absolute value function \(|\theta_j|\) is non-differentiable at \(\theta_j = 0\), Lasso has no closed-form analytical solution and is solved numerically via coordinate descent.

3.1 Geometric Comparison of \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) Norms in 2D

To visualize why Lasso produces exact zeros (sparsity) while Ridge does not, consider all points in two dimensions at unit distance (\(\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\| = 1\)) from the origin:

  • \(L_2\) Norm Unit Contour: \(\{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 = 1\}\) forms a smooth circle.
  • \(L_1\) Norm Unit Contour: \(\{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^2 : |\theta_1| + |\theta_2| = 1\}\) forms a diamond with sharp corners at \((\pm 1, 0)\) and \((0, \pm 1)\) directly on the coordinate axes.

4.1 Comparison of Fitted Parameter Weights: OLS, Ridge, and Lasso

To understand how regularization transforms model complexity, we inspect the learned parameter weights across all 16 polynomial terms (\(u^0, u^1, \dots, u^{15}\)), where \(u^0 = 1\) represents the intercept:

  • Unpenalized Intercept (\(u^0\)): In both Ridge and Lasso, the intercept \(\theta_0\) (\(u^0\)) is deliberately unpenalized, ensuring that the model remains invariant to constant vertical shifts in \(y\). Notice that \(u^0\) remains active across all four models, anchoring the prediction to the mean level of the observations.
  • Ordinary Least Squares (\(\lambda = 0\)): Without regularization, the unconstrained estimator fits sample noise, driving adjacent monomial weights into opposing billions (\(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_2 \approx 1.83 \times 10^{10}\)), identical to the weight explosion seen in Lecture 04.
  • Ridge Regularization (\(\lambda = 0.1\)): The \(L_2\) penalty continuously shrinks all 15 feature weights simultaneously by nine orders of magnitude (\(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_2 \approx 9.27\)). Crucially, all 16 parameters remain non-zero—Ridge achieves shrinkage, not sparsity.
  • Lasso Regularization (\(\lambda = 10^{-3}\), Mild): Because the \(L_1\) diamond ball has sharp corners aligned with the coordinate axes, Lasso drives redundant coefficients strictly to zero. At mild penalty, 11 of the 15 feature powers are pruned to zero, leaving active weights on the intercept \(u^0\) and the low-degree terms (\(u^1, u^2, u^3\)) that capture the underlying parabolic signal.
  • Lasso Regularization (\(\lambda = 0.1\), Strong): At the exact same regularization strength as Ridge (\(\lambda = 0.1\)), Lasso sets 13 of the 15 feature weights to exactly zero, isolating only the intercept \(u^0\) and the dominant curvature features (\(u^2\) and \(u^3\)).

5. Summary & Key Takeaways

  1. Residual Error Decomposition: Total Mean Squared Error decomposes into \(\text{MSE} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2\) because the bias vector \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{bias}}\), variance vector \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{var}}\), and noise vector \(\varepsilon\) are mutually orthogonal in expectation.
  2. \(L_2\) Regularization (Ridge): Adds \(\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2\) to the loss, continuously shrinking parameter weights and shifting the Gram matrix eigenvalues to guarantee invertibility \((\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\).
  3. \(L_1\) Regularization (Lasso): Adds \(\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1\), leveraging the sharp corners of the diamond unit ball to perform automatic feature selection by setting uninformative weights strictly to zero.
  4. Controlling High Degrees: Regularization allows us to use rich hypothesis spaces (such as high-degree polynomials) while completely preventing the explosive oscillations characteristic of unconstrained Ordinary Least Squares.

### 1.1 Decomposition of the Residual Error

::: {.callout-note}
### Mathematical Setup & Random Variables

| Quantity | Variable / Expression | Expectation & Properties |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Truth** | $f(x)$ | $\mathbb{E}[f(x)] = f(x)$ (unknown,deterministic function of $x$ and possibly further variables) |
| **Noise** | $\varepsilon$ | $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = \sigma^2$ |
| **Observed Target** | $Y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ | $\mathbb{E}[Y] = f(x) + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = f(x)$ |
| **Model Prediction** | $\hat{Y}$ | $\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]$  (expected model prediction across training datasets) |
| **Residual Error** | $Y - \hat{Y}$ | $\mathbb{E}[Y - \hat{Y}]$  (expected difference between target and prediction |

:::

#### Decomposition of the Residual Error

The residual error, $Y - \hat{Y}$, is a random variable that can be decomposed into three distinct components: **Bias**, **Variance**, and **Irreducible Noise**:

$$\begin{aligned}
Y - \hat{Y} &= \big(f(x) + \varepsilon\big) - \hat{Y} \\
&= \underbrace{\big(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]\big)}_{B} + \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}] - \hat{Y}\big)}_{V} + \varepsilon \\
&= B + V + \varepsilon
\end{aligned}$$

where:

* **bias** $B = f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}]$ measures the systematic deviation between the true underlying mechanism $f(x)$ and the expected prediction of the model across datasets. Notice that $B$ is a deterministic constant.
* **variance** $V = \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}] - \hat{Y}$ captures the sample-to-sample fluctuation of the estimator $\hat{Y}$ around its own expectation (with $\mathbb{E}[V] = 0$).
* $\varepsilon = Y - f(x)$ is the **irreducible noise** in the target observation (with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$).

#### Mean Squared Error and the Pythagorean Relation

The Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the expected squared residual error:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{MSE} & = \mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2]  \\
           & = \mathbb{E}\left[(B + V + \varepsilon)^2\right] \\
           & = \mathbb{E}[B^2] + \mathbb{E}[V^2] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] \\
&\quad + 2\,\mathbb{E}[B V] + 2\,\mathbb{E}[B \varepsilon] + 2\,\mathbb{E}[V \varepsilon]
\end{aligned}$$

All three cross-terms vanish:

1. **Bias and Variance:** Because $B$ is a deterministic constant, it factors out of the expectation:
   $$\mathbb{E}[B V] = B \cdot \mathbb{E}[V] = B \cdot \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}] - \hat{Y}] = B \cdot 0 = 0$$

2. **Bias and Noise:** Because $B$ is constant and the noise has zero mean:
   $$\mathbb{E}[B \varepsilon] = B \cdot \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = B \cdot 0 = 0$$

3. **Variance and Noise:** Because the test noise $\varepsilon$ is *independent* of the training data used to form $\hat{Y}$:
   $$\mathbb{E}[V \varepsilon] = \mathbb{E}[V] \cdot \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0$$


::: {.callout-note}
#### Pythagoras again: Orthogonality of Random Variables

If we define the inner product of two random variables as $\langle U, W \rangle = \mathbb{E}[U W]$, then $\mathbb{E}[U W] = 0$ is equivalent to orthogonality of the two random variables. So the decomposition of the MSE is reminiscent of the Pythagorean theorem.
:::


$$\boxed{\text{MSE} = \mathbb{E}[(Y - \hat{Y})^2] = \underbrace{(f(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}])^2}_{\text{Bias}^2(\hat{Y})} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{Y} - \mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}])^2\right]}_{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{Y})} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{Irreducible Noise}}}$$

## 2. $L_2$ Regularization (Ridge)

Because irreducible noise $\sigma^2$ is an unchangeable property, reducing total prediction error requires balancing Bias and Variance:

* **Standard OLS** fits training data without constraints. For flexible models (such as high-degree polynomials), OLS achieves zero or near-zero training bias ($\text{Bias}^2 \approx 0$), but can result in large **Variance** $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{Y})$ where predictions swing wildly from one sample (e.g. patient cohort) to another.

* **The Regularization Principle:** Rather than insisting on strict unbiasedness, the goal of regularization is to **deliberately accept a small, controlled amount of Bias** by constraining parameter magnitudes, in exchange for a **large reduction in Variance**.

By accepting a slight bias, we stabilize the estimator against sample-to-sample volatility and achieve a substantially lower overall Mean Squared Error.

### 2.1 The Ridge Estimator

**Ridge Regression ($L_2$ Regularization)** operationalizes this principle by adding a penalty on the squared Euclidean norm of the parameter vector to the loss function:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$$

where:

* $\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\theta})^2$ is the standard Residual Sum of Squares (RSS).

* $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^p \theta_j^2$ is the squared Euclidean norm of the parameter vector.

* $\lambda \ge 0$ is the **regularization hyperparameter**:

  * $\lambda = 0$: Recovers standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS).
  * $\lambda \to \infty$: Drives all weights toward zero ($\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \to \mathbf{0}$).

Minimizing the regularized loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ yields the modified normal equations in closed form:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{Ridge}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$$

Notice the algebraic effect of $\lambda\mathbf{I}$:

* When model space is large, the Gram matrix $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ is near-singular and poorly conditioned.

* Adding $\lambda\mathbf{I}$ shifts every diagonal entry by $+\lambda$, guaranteeing that $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})$ is strictly positive-definite and invertible for any $\lambda > 0$.


---

## 3. $L_1$ Regularization (Lasso)

While Ridge shrinks coefficients continuously toward zero, it rarely drives any parameter strictly to zero. **Lasso Regression ($L_1$ Regularization)** replaces the squared penalty with the sum of absolute values:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lasso}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1 = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p |\theta_j|$$

Because the absolute value function $|\theta_j|$ is non-differentiable at $\theta_j = 0$, Lasso has no closed-form analytical solution and is solved numerically via coordinate descent.

### 3.1 Geometric Comparison of $L_1$ and $L_2$ Norms in 2D

To visualize why Lasso produces exact zeros (sparsity) while Ridge does not, consider all points in two dimensions at unit distance ($\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\| = 1$) from the origin:

* **$L_2$ Norm Unit Contour:** $\{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^2 : \theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2 = 1\}$ forms a smooth circle.
* **$L_1$ Norm Unit Contour:** $\{\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^2 : |\theta_1| + |\theta_2| = 1\}$ forms a diamond with sharp corners at $(\pm 1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm 1)$ directly on the coordinate axes.

Notice that $u^0$ remains active across all four models, anchoring the prediction to the mean level of the observations.

## 5. Summary & Key Takeaways

1. **Residual Error Decomposition:** Total Mean Squared Error decomposes into $\text{MSE} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2$ because the bias vector $\mathbf{v}_{\text{bias}}$, variance vector $\mathbf{v}_{\text{var}}$, and noise vector $\varepsilon$ are mutually orthogonal in expectation.
2. **$L_2$ Regularization (Ridge):** Adds $\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^2$ to the loss, continuously shrinking parameter weights and shifting the Gram matrix eigenvalues to guarantee invertibility $(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}$.
3. **$L_1$ Regularization (Lasso):** Adds $\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1$, leveraging the sharp corners of the diamond unit ball to perform automatic feature selection by setting uninformative weights strictly to zero.
4. **Controlling High Degrees:** Regularization allows us to use rich hypothesis spaces (such as high-degree polynomials) while completely preventing the explosive oscillations characteristic of unconstrained Ordinary Least Squares.
