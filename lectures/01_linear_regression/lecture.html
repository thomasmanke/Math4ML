1. Medical Motivation

We previously analyzed paired observations of Carbohydrate Intake \(x\) (g) and Blood Glucose Response \(y\) (mg/dL) for \(n = 2\) patients using a system of linear equations:

\[\begin{array}{c|c} y & x \\ \hline y_1 & x_1 \\ y_2 & x_2 \end{array} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \begin{aligned} y_1 &= \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 \\ y_2 &= \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_2 \end{aligned} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \theta_0 \\ \theta_1 \end{bmatrix}\]

  • With \(n = 2\) observations and \(p = 2\) parameters (\(\theta_0, \theta_1\)), the design matrix \(X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \end{bmatrix}\) is a square \(2 \times 2\) matrix.

  • An exact inverse \(X^{-1}\) exists, passing a line perfectly through both points with zero residual error.

Note\(n = 2 \to n = 100\) ?

Do you think an exact linear solution will still be possible, if we have data for \(n=100\) patients?

\(n = 100\) Patients in Matrix Form

  • Data Matrix Dimensions: \[X \in \mathbb{R}^{100 \times 2}, \quad y \in \mathbb{R}^{100 \times 1}, \quad \theta = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_0 \\ \theta_1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 1}\]

  • Same Matrix Equation: The structural algebraic equation remains completely intact—only the sample dimension \(n\) changes: \[y = X\theta\]

  • The Rectangular Dilemma: \(X\) is \(100 \times 2\). We cannot compute \(X^{-1}\) directly because \(X\) is not square!

  • Biological Noise & Error: Patient responses vary due to metabolism, genetics, and measurement timing: \[y = X\theta + e\]

2. The Gram Trick

What happens if we take the rectangular system \(y = X\theta\) and multiply both sides on the left by \(X^T\)?

\[X^T y = X^T X \theta \implies \hat{\theta} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y\]

  • Squaring the System: Multiplying \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{100 \times 2}\) by its transpose \(X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 100}\) converts the rectangular system into a square, symmetric \(2 \times 2\) Gram matrix (\(X^T X\)).

  • The Pragmatic Solution: Assuming \(X^T X\) is invertible, left-multiplication by \(X^T\) immediately yields a concrete solution \(\hat{\theta}\).

NoteWhat is the origin of this trick?
  • minimizing losses
  • maximizing likelihood

ax.set_xlabel('Carbohydrate intake [g]', fontsize=11)
ax.set_ylabel('Blood glucose response [mg/dL]', fontsize=11)
ax.set_title('Baseline Model (Mean Blood Glucose) & Residual Errors', fontsize=12, pad=12)
ax.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.5)
ax.legend(loc='lower right', frameon=True)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

We define the Residual Sum of Squares \(\text{RSS}(\theta)\) as the squared \(L_2\) norm of the error vector \(e\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{RSS}(\theta) &= \|e\|^2 = e^T e = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 \\ &= (y - X\theta)^T (y - X\theta) \qquad \longrightarrow \qquad \text{minimize} \end{aligned}\]

Notice that RSS is a simple number (a scalar product).

Deriving the OLS Estimator

To find the optimal parameter vector \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}}\), we minimize \(\text{RSS}(\theta)\) by setting its gradient to zero:

NoteStep-by-Step Derivation

  1. Expand the Residual Sum of Squares \(\text{RSS}(\theta)\): \[\begin{aligned} \text{RSS}(\theta) &= e^T e = (y - X\theta)^T (y - X\theta) \\ &= y^T y - y^T X \theta - \theta^T X^T y + \theta^T X^T X \theta \\ &= y^T y - 2 \theta^T X^T y + \theta^T X^T X \theta \end{aligned}\] (Notice that \(y^T X \theta = \theta^T X^T y\) is a scalar).

  2. Compute the gradient vector with respect to \(\theta\): \[\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta \text{RSS}(\theta) &= -2 X^T y + 2 X^T X \theta \\ &= -2 X^T (y - X\theta) = -2 X^T e \end{aligned}\] (\(\text{RSS}(\theta)\) is a scalar and the gradient \(\nabla_\theta \text{RSS}(\theta)\) has the same dimensions as \(\theta\)).

  3. Set the gradient to zero (First-Order Necessary Condition): \[-2 X^T e = 0 \iff X^T X \theta = X^T y \quad \text{(Normal Equations)}\]

  4. Solve for \(\theta\) assuming \(C = X^T X\) is invertible: \[\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}} = C^{-1} X^T y \quad \text{where } C = X^T X\]

  5. Dimensions Summary: \[\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times 1}, \quad C = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}, \quad C^{-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}, \quad X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}, \quad y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}\]

\[\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}} = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y\]

4. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Probabilistic Modeling of Noise

Why do we minimize squared errors (\(L_2\) loss)? Assume residual errors \(e_i\) are independent Gaussian noise:

\[e_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \implies y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(x_i^T \theta, \sigma^2)\]

The Likelihood Function \(L(\theta)\)

Assuming the model is correct, the probability of a single observation \(y_i\) is:

\[p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\]

Assuming independent observations, the joint likelihood of observing all \(n\) patient responses is the product over all observations:

\[L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)\]

Maximizing Log-Likelihood (\(\text{MLE}\))

Taking the natural log turns the product into a sum:

\[\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2\]

Maximizing \(\ell(\theta)\) with respect to \(\theta\):

\[\arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta) \iff \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2 = \arg\min_\theta \|y - X\theta\|^2\]

Takeaway: Under Gaussian noise, Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) yields the exact same estimator as OLS!

5. Geometric Derivation

Instead of calculus, we can derive OLS purely using Linear Algebra & Vector Projections in observation space (\(\mathbb{R}^n\)):

  • Observation Space (\(\mathbb{R}^n\)): The target vector \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\) lives in \(n\)-dimensional observation space (\(n=100\)). The origin \(\mathbf{0}\) represents zero response for all patients.

  • Model Subspace (\(\text{col}(X)\)): All possible predictions \(\hat{y}\) are linear combinations of the columns of \(X\): \[\begin{bmatrix} \hat{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{y}_n \end{bmatrix} = \theta_0 \mathbf{1}_n + \theta_1 \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}\] where \(\mathbf{1}_n\) denotes the \(n \times 1\) column vector of ones. All possible parameter combinations \((\theta_0, \theta_1)\) trace out the column space \(\text{col}(X)\), and any model prediction \(\hat{y}\) is simply an element of this subspace.

  • Orthogonal Projection: The point \(\hat{y} \in \text{col}(X)\) closest to \(y\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto \(\text{col}(X)\).

Orthogonality Condition

The residual error vector \(e = y - \hat{y} = y - X\hat{\theta}\) must drop at a right angle (\(\perp\)) onto \(\text{col}(X)\):

\[e \perp \text{col}(X) \iff X^T e = 0\]

Substituting \(e = y - X\hat{\theta}\):

\[X^T (y - X\hat{\theta}) = 0 \implies X^T y - X^T X \hat{\theta} = 0 \implies X^T X \hat{\theta} = X^T y\]

Conclusion: Both Calculus (\(\nabla_\theta \text{RSS} = 0\)) and Geometry (\(e \perp \text{col}(X)\)) yield the exact same Normal Equations!

6. Model Predictions

With the optimal parameters \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}}\), we compute predicted responses \(\hat{y}\) for our cohort:

\[\hat{y} = X\hat{\theta} = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T y = H y \quad \text{where } H = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T\]

  • The Hat Matrix (\(H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\)): \(H\) transforms observed responses \(y\) directly into fitted predictions \(\hat{y}\) (“putting a hat on \(y\)”).
  • Projection Operator: The hat matrix \(H\) is symmetric (\(H^T = H\)) and acts as a projection operator that maps any vector from observation space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) onto the model subspace \(\text{col}(X)\), as illustrated in the geometric projection figure.

7. History of Regression

8. Summary

  • Matrix power: \(y = X\theta\)
  • Modesty: \(y = X\theta + \varepsilon\)
  • Optimism: \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) or \(\varepsilon \perp \hat{y}\)
  • Solution: \(\theta = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y\)
  • Predictions: \(\hat{y} = P y\), where \(H = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T\) is the hat matrix (\(P = H\)).

## 4. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

### Probabilistic Modeling of Noise

Why do we minimize squared errors ($L_2$ loss)? Assume residual errors $e_i$ are independent Gaussian noise:

$$e_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \implies y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(x_i^T \theta, \sigma^2)$$

---

### The Likelihood Function $L(\theta)$

Assuming the model is correct, the probability of a single observation $y_i$ is:

$$p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$$

Assuming independent observations, the joint likelihood of observing all $n$ patient responses is the product over all observations:

$$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$$

---

### Maximizing Log-Likelihood ($\text{MLE}$)

Taking the natural log turns the product into a sum:

$$\ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2$$

Maximizing $\ell(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$:

$$\arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta) \iff \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^T\theta)^2 = \arg\min_\theta \|y - X\theta\|^2$$

> **Takeaway:** Under Gaussian noise, Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) yields the **exact same estimator** as OLS!

## 5. Geometric Derivation

Instead of calculus, we can derive OLS purely using **Linear Algebra & Vector Projections** in observation space ($\mathbb{R}^n$):

* **Observation Space ($\mathbb{R}^n$):**
  The target vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ lives in $n$-dimensional observation space ($n=100$). The origin $\mathbf{0}$ represents zero response for all patients.

* **Model Subspace ($\text{col}(X)$):**
  All possible predictions $\hat{y}$ are linear combinations of the columns of $X$:
  $$\begin{bmatrix} \hat{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \hat{y}_n \end{bmatrix} = \theta_0 \mathbf{1}_n + \theta_1 \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}$$
  where $\mathbf{1}_n$ denotes the $n \times 1$ column vector of ones. All possible parameter combinations $(\theta_0, \theta_1)$ trace out the column space $\text{col}(X)$, and any model prediction $\hat{y}$ is simply an element of this subspace.

* **Orthogonal Projection:**
  The point $\hat{y} \in \text{col}(X)$ closest to $y$ is the **orthogonal projection** of $y$ onto $\text{col}(X)$.

![Geometric Projection View of OLS](images/ols_geometry_2d.png){width="85%" fig-align="center"}

---

### Orthogonality Condition

The residual error vector $e = y - \hat{y} = y - X\hat{\theta}$ must drop at a **right angle ($\perp$)** onto $\text{col}(X)$:

$$e \perp \text{col}(X) \iff X^T e = 0$$

Substituting $e = y - X\hat{\theta}$:

$$X^T (y - X\hat{\theta}) = 0 \implies X^T y - X^T X \hat{\theta} = 0 \implies X^T X \hat{\theta} = X^T y$$

> **Conclusion:** Both Calculus ($\nabla_\theta \text{RSS} = 0$) and Geometry ($e \perp \text{col}(X)$) yield the **exact same Normal Equations**!

## 6. Model Predictions

With the optimal parameters $\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}}$, we compute predicted responses $\hat{y}$ for our cohort:

$$\hat{y} = X\hat{\theta} = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T y = H y \quad \text{where } H = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T$$

* **The Hat Matrix ($H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$):** $H$ transforms observed responses $y$ directly into fitted predictions $\hat{y}$ ("putting a hat on $y$").
* **Projection Operator:** The hat matrix $H$ is symmetric ($H^T = H$) and acts as a projection operator that maps any vector from observation space $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto the model subspace $\text{col}(X)$, as illustrated in the geometric projection figure.

## 8. Summary

* **Matrix power:** $y = X\theta$
* **Modesty:** $y = X\theta + \varepsilon$
* **Optimism:** $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ or $\varepsilon \perp \hat{y}$
* **Solution:** $\theta = (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y$
* **Predictions:** $\hat{y} = P y$, where $H = X(X^T X)^{-1} X^T$ is the hat matrix ($P = H$).
