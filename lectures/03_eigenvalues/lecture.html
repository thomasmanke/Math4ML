03 Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

Eigenartige Eigenwerte: Directions of No Rotation

Author

Thomas Manke

Published

September 13, 2026

1. Preparation & Concept Overview

Pre-Class Preparation (Flipped Classroom)

Before coming to this lecture, please complete the following:

The Zero Rotation Hook

  • The Intriguing Question:
    • When we multiply a vector by a matrix, it changes both its length and its direction (it rotates).
    • Is there any vector that does not rotate at all?
  • The Concept of Zero Rotation:
    • Eigenvectors are the special directions in space that remain on their original axis under a linear transformation.
    • They are only stretched or shrunk by a scaling factor \(\lambda\) (the eigenvalue).
    • David Hilbert (1904) coined the German term “Eigen” (own/characteristic) because these directions belong intrinsically to the transformation.

2. Real-World Applications

Where do eigenvectors define our decisions?

  • Medicine & Health:
    • Analyzing 3D anatomical shapes (e.g. alignment of organs in MRI scans).
    • Eigenvalues identify the primary directions of shape variation across patient groups.
  • Society & Opinion Dynamics:
    • Social influence models (DeGroot model) represent opinion updates as matrix multiplications.
    • The second largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\) measures how fast a society reaches consensus versus staying polarized.

3. History & Philosophy

Behind every formula are people, historical contexts, and ethical reflections:

Principal Axes of Inertia

  • Euler & Lagrange (18th Century):
    • Studied the rotation of rigid bodies.
    • Discovered that an object only rotates stably without “wobbling” around specific axes.

David Hilbert (1904)

  • Coined Eigenwert and Eigenvektor to study differential equations.

4. Theory: Summary

Recap: [Linear transformations, determinants, matrix rank]

  • Fundamental Eigenvalue Equation: \[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}, \quad \mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}\]
  • Characteristic Equation: \[(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = 0\]
  • Diagonalization & Eigenbasis: \[\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}\]
  • Note: Analytical \(2 \times 2\) matrix proofs and power iteration convergence are provided in the companion derivation notes / whiteboard cheat sheet.

5. Implementations and Illustrations

Computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors using NumPy:

import numpy as np

# A symmetric matrix A representing scaling along x=y
A = np.array([
    [2, 1],
    [1, 2]
])

# Compute eigenvalues (L) and eigenvectors (V)
L, V = np.linalg.eig(A)

print("Eigenvalues:", L)          # Output: [3. 1.]
print("Eigenvector 1:", V[:, 0]) # Output: [ 0.707  0.707] (scaled v1)
print("Eigenvector 2:", V[:, 1]) # Output: [-0.707  0.707] (scaled v2)

6. Outlook & Optional Extensions

Key Takeaways

  • Eigenvectors define invariant coordinate axes where linear transformations act purely as scalar scaling.

Optional Extensions

  • Complex Spectra: If a matrix is asymmetric, its eigenvalues can be complex numbers (representing rotation + scaling).
  • Infinite Dimensions: In quantum mechanics, operators act on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces; eigenvalues represent discrete energy levels.
