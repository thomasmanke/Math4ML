Transformations and Normalizations
Author

Thomas Manke

Published

October 26, 2026

Feature engineering encompasses the mathematical preprocessing steps applied to raw features prior to fitting a statistical model. While raw observational data directly capture physical or clinical phenomena in their native measurement units, they frequently present challenges: disparate numerical scales, extreme skews, heavy tails, and heteroscedasticity.

Two fundamental classes of preprocessing address these challenges in distinct ways:

  1. Transformations (Changing Shape): Nonlinear mappings that compress or stretch relative distances, reshape distributions toward symmetry, and reduce the leverage of extreme observations.
  2. Normalization & Standardization (Changing Scale): Affine linear mappings that shift the location and rescale the spread of features without altering the underlying shape of the distribution.

1. Transformations: Changing Shape

Nonlinear transformations alter the geometric shape of feature distributions. Crucially, transformations must be applied prior to standardization.

Mathematical Formulations

  • Square Root Transformation: \[x^\prime = \sqrt{x}\]
  • Logarithmic Transformation: \[x^\prime = \log\left(\frac{x}{u_0} + 1\right)\] where \(u_0\) represents the characteristic reference unit of the measurement (e.g., \(1\,\mu\text{U/ml}\) for insulin), rendering the argument dimensionless.

Why Transform?

  • Dampening Influential Outliers & Leverage: In linear regression, parameter estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = (\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}\) are highly sensitive to observations far from the mean in feature space (high leverage points). For right-skewed variables such as insulin, a few extreme observations can dominate the sum of squared residuals (\(RSS\)). The concave nature of \(\log(x)\) compresses extreme values, pulling outliers closer to the median and stabilizing parameter estimates.
  • Stabilizing Variance (Homoscedasticity): Standard linear regression assumes constant residual variance across all values of the predictor: \(\mathrm{Var}(\epsilon_i \mid x_i) = \sigma^2\). For Poisson-like count processes (such as pregnancies), the variance scales proportionally with the mean: \(\mathrm{Var}(X) \propto \mathbb{E}[X]\). The variance-stabilizing transformation for a Poisson process is the square root: \[\mathrm{Var}(\sqrt{X}) \approx \frac{1}{4}\]
  • Linearizing Relationships: Many biological processes obey power-law or exponential kinetics (\(y \propto x^\alpha\)). Log-transforming the variables converts multiplicative relationships into additive, linear equations amenable to ordinary least squares: \[\log(y) = \log(c) + \alpha \log(x)\]

Why Not Transform Everything?

Transformations should not be applied arbitrarily across all features:

  • Symmetric Physiological Variables: Variables such as blood_pressure, glucose, skin_thickness, and bmi are already relatively symmetric around biological setpoints. Applying a concave logarithmic transform to an already symmetric distribution creates an artificial negative (left) skew, degrading model fit.
  • Interval Variables & Demographic Metrics: Features like age have a uniform physical and epidemiological interpretation (e.g., risk change per year of age). Transforming age compresses the elderly age groups relative to young adults, distorting demographic comparability. The positive skew in age simply reflects the recruited cohort (younger adults in this study) rather than an unconstrained physical process.

2. Normalization & Standardization: Changing Scale

Standardization rescales variables to a common numerical range without altering the relative distances between percentiles or the underlying distributional shape.

Mathematical Formulation

The standard score (\(z\)-score) is defined as:

\[z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\]

where \(\mu = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\) is the sample mean and \(\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2}\) is the sample standard deviation.

Affine Invariance & Properties

  • Affine Linear Transformation: Standardization is strictly an affine linear transformation of the form \(z = a x + b\), where \(a = 1/\sigma\) and \(b = -\mu/\sigma\).
  • Shape Invariance: Because affine mappings preserve ratios of lengths, standardization does not alter the skewness, kurtosis, or modality of the distribution. A skewed feature remains equally skewed after standardization; only its mean (\(0\)) and standard deviation (\(1\)) change.

Why Standardize?

  • Numerical Stability: When features differ by orders of magnitude (e.g., insulin in hundreds vs. pedigree in fractions of a unit), the Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) can become ill-conditioned, leading to numerical errors when computing inverses. Centering and scaling maintains a well-conditioned matrix.
  • Preserving Sample Distances: Distance metrics such as the Euclidean distance: \[d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^p (x_{ik} - x_{jk})^2}\] are dominated by whichever feature has the largest raw numerical variance. Standardization ensures each feature contributes proportionally to geometric distances in clustering (k-means), nearest neighbors (k-NN), and Principal Component Analysis (PCA).
  • Optimization & Regularization: In gradient descent, disparate feature scales cause the loss surface to take the form of elongated, eccentric ellipsoids, forcing optimization trajectories to oscillate inefficiently. Standardization produces circular/spherical level sets, allowing gradient updates to point directly toward the minimum. Furthermore, regularized loss functions (such as Ridge \(\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2^2\) and Lasso \(\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_1\)) apply an isotropic penalty to coefficients; unstandardized features would result in arbitrary, unfair penalization dictated entirely by measurement units.

3. Comparison Across Preprocessing Stages

The three-panel violin plot below illustrates the progressive impact of targeted preprocessing on the physiological features of the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset:

  1. Raw, Unscaled Features: Dynamic ranges vary dramatically across native clinical measurement units (insulin reaches nearly 800, while pedigree remains below 2.5).
  2. Standardized Features (\(z\)-scores): All eight features share zero mean and unit variance, making distributions comparable on a shared scale, but skews remain unchanged.
  3. Targeted Transformations & Standardized Features: Targeted transformations are applied only where mathematically and clinically justified: square root for count-based pregnancies and logarithmic for heavy-tailed insulin. Naturally symmetric features (blood_pressure, glucose, skin_thickness, bmi, pedigree, age) are left untransformed, and all features are then standardized to \(z\)-scores.
Code 
# Stage 1: Raw features
df_raw = df_clean[feature_cols]

# Stage 2: Standardized features (affine transform: preserves shape)
df_z = (df_raw - df_raw.mean()) / df_raw.std()

# Stage 3: Targeted shape transformations, followed by standardization
df_targeted = df_raw.copy()
# Variance stabilization for Poisson count process
df_targeted["pregnancies"] = np.sqrt(df_targeted["pregnancies"])
# Outlier compression for heavy-tailed biological concentration
df_targeted["insulin"] = np.log1p(df_targeted["insulin"])
# Other features (glucose, blood_pressure, skin_thickness, bmi, pedigree, age) remain untransformed

df_targeted_z = (df_targeted - df_targeted.mean()) / df_targeted.std()

NoteKey Insights: Targeted Transformations, Nondimensionalization, and Power Transforms
  • Targeted Selection vs. Arbitrary Transformation: Transformations should be a targeted, domain-motivated choice rather than an automated default. Variables that are already relatively symmetric around biological setpoints (blood_pressure, glucose, skin_thickness, bmi) do not benefit from logarithmic compression; transforming them introduces an unnecessary negative skew. Similarly, interval variables such as age preserve chronological and epidemiological meaning in their linear form.
  • Requirement for Dimensionless Numbers: Mathematical transcendental functions (such as logarithms and exponentials) are rigorously defined only on pure, dimensionless numbers. When transforming a dimensionful physical measurement \(x\) with unit \([x]\) (e.g., serum insulin in \(\mu\text{U/ml}\)), division by a reference unit \(u_0 = 1\,[x]\) renders the quantity dimensionless (\(x^\prime = x / u_0\)) before applying \(\log(x^\prime + 1)\).
  • Generalized Power Transformations (Box-Cox): Many non-linear transformations belong to parameterized families. In particular, the Box-Cox Power Transformation generalizes power and logarithmic transforms for positive variables: \[y^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \frac{y^\lambda - 1}{\lambda} & \text{if } \lambda \neq 0 \\ \ln(y) & \text{if } \lambda = 0 \end{cases}\] The parameter \(\lambda\) smoothly bridges between square root (\(\lambda = 0.5\)), natural logarithm (\(\lambda \to 0\)), and an untransformed linear shift (\(\lambda = 1\)). While automated optimization of \(\lambda\) can be useful in exploratory modeling, targeted transformations based on physical and biological principles remain the foundation of interpretable feature engineering.
CautionCautious Note: Parameter Interpretation with Preprocessed Features

Interpreting regression coefficients (slopes \(\theta_1\) and intercepts \(\theta_0\)) in original physical or clinical units requires reversing any applied transformations and standardizations:

  • Standardized Features: When \(x\) and \(y\) are standardized, the slope \(\beta_1\) represents a dimensionless effect size: the expected standard deviation change in \(y\) per standard deviation change in \(x\).
  • Log-Transformed Features: When \(x\) is log-transformed (\(x^\prime = \log x\)), the slope \(\theta_1\) measures relative elasticity: a 1% relative increase in \(x\) corresponds to an expected change of approximately \(0.01 \times \theta_1\) in \(y\).
  • Reversing Transformations: Before communicating predictive equations or baseline risks back to clinicians or decision-makers, model outputs must be mapped back to their original physical units (\(x = u_0(\exp(x^\prime) - 1)\)) to restore direct clinical interpretability.

## 2. Normalization & Standardization: Changing Scale

Standardization rescales variables to a common numerical range without altering the relative distances between percentiles or the underlying distributional shape.

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

* **Affine Linear Transformation**:
  Standardization is strictly an affine linear transformation of the form $z = a x + b$, where $a = 1/\sigma$ and $b = -\mu/\sigma$. 
* **Shape Invariance**:
  Because affine mappings preserve ratios of lengths, **standardization does not alter the skewness, kurtosis, or modality** of the distribution. A skewed feature remains equally skewed after standardization; only its mean ($0$) and standard deviation ($1$) change.

* **Numerical Stability**:
