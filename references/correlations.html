Variable Relationships

To quantify associations between paired continuous variables, we evaluate two complementary correlation measures:

  1. Pearson Correlation Coefficient (\(r_{xy} \in [-1, 1]\)): Measures strictly linear association: \[r_{xy} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, y)}{s_x s_y}\] In vector form with mean-centered vectors \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \bar{x}\mathbf{1}\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}\): \[r_{xy} = \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^T \tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2 \|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2} = \cos(\phi)\]

  2. Spearman Rank Correlation (\(r_s \in [-1, 1]\)): Evaluates monotonic association by computing Pearson’s formula on the ranks \(R(x)\) and \(R(y)\): \[r_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}, \qquad d_i = R(x_i) - R(y_i)\] Spearman’s \(r_s\) assesses whether variables increase together regardless of whether the trend is linear, and is substantially more robust to extreme outliers.

Code 
x = df_clean['insulin']
y = df_clean['glucose']
r_pearson = x.corr(y, method='pearson')
r_spearman = x.corr(y, method='spearman')

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.5))

# Panel 1: Raw Observation Space (Pearson)
axes[0].scatter(x, y, alpha=0.45, color='#1f77b4', edgecolors='none', s=35)
axes[0].set_title(f'Raw Clinical Space: Pearson $r = {r_pearson:.3f}$', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
axes[0].set_xlabel('Insulin [µU/ml]')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Glucose [mg/dL]')
axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# Panel 2: Rank Space (Spearman)
rx = x.rank()
ry = y.rank()
axes[1].scatter(rx, ry, alpha=0.45, color='#2ca02c', edgecolors='none', s=35)
axes[1].set_title(f'Rank Space: Spearman $r_s = {r_spearman:.3f}$', fontsize=11, fontweight='bold')
axes[1].set_xlabel('Rank(Insulin)')
axes[1].set_ylabel('Rank(Glucose)')
axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
NotePearson versus Spearman
  • Sensitivity to High-Leverage Outliers: Pearson correlation operates on squared Euclidean distances. Extreme insulin observations (reaching up to \(846\ \mu\text{U/ml}\)) pull on the linear fit and attenuate sample covariance.
  • Biological Saturation: Cellular glucose response naturally exhibits saturation (diminishing return at high insulin concentrations). Rank transformation straightens monotonic curvature, bounding extreme outliers and capturing the true strength of biological co-regulation.
  • Attention — Single Summary Metrics: Both numbers are just single scalar values attempting to summarize potentially complex bivariate data structures. Neither number should be considered “true”, and both have distinct limitations:
    • Ties and excessive zeros: When datasets contain many identical zero values (e.g., missing records, detection limits, or single-cell dropouts), rank assignment can heavily skew Spearman’s rank correlation.
    • Isolated extreme outliers: A single extreme bivariate outlier can heavily inflate or deflate sample covariance, creating the illusion of a very strong or falsely absent Pearson correlation. Always inspect the bivariate scatter plot rather than relying blindly on a single correlation coefficient.

TipLink to Multicollinearity in Regression

When two predictor columns exhibit high pairwise correlation (\(|r| \approx 1\)), their columns in the design matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) are nearly linearly dependent. This causes the Gram matrix \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) to approach singularity (\(\det(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}) \approx 0\)), inflating parameter estimation variance in \((\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})^{-1}\).

## Variable Relationships

To quantify associations between paired continuous variables, we evaluate two complementary correlation measures:

1. **Pearson Correlation Coefficient ($r_{xy} \in [-1, 1]$)**: Measures strictly **linear** association:
   $$r_{xy} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x, y)}{s_x s_y}$$
   In vector form with mean-centered vectors $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \bar{x}\mathbf{1}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y} - \bar{y}\mathbf{1}$:
   $$r_{xy} = \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^T \tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2 \|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2} = \cos(\phi)$$

2. **Spearman Rank Correlation ($r_s \in [-1, 1]$)**: Evaluates **monotonic** association by computing Pearson's formula on the *ranks* $R(x)$ and $R(y)$:
   $$r_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}, \qquad d_i = R(x_i) - R(y_i)$$
   Spearman's $r_s$ assesses whether variables increase together regardless of whether the trend is linear, and is substantially more robust to extreme outliers.

```{python}
#| label: fig-pearson-vs-spearman
#| fig-cap: "Insulin vs. Glucose: Comparing linear Pearson correlation in raw clinical space (left) with monotonic Spearman rank correlation in rank space (right)."
#| code-fold: true

x = df_clean['insulin']
y = df_clean['glucose']
r_pearson = x.corr(y, method='pearson')
r_spearman = x.corr(y, method='spearman')

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.5))

axes[0].set_xlabel('Insulin [µU/ml]')
axes[0].set_ylabel('Glucose [mg/dL]')
axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# Panel 2: Rank Space (Spearman)

ry = y.rank()

axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
```
